# Design notes

- **Shape first.** Each distinct control gets a recognizable, different filled-button silhouette, not a glyph in a circular badge. Allow smoothly varied fruit-like asymmetry while keeping functional landmarks clear. The open-folder arrow extends beyond the folder edge; distinguish add-folder from a plain folder as well.
- **Flat back, dimensional top.** The button has a flat supported back, while its top has a rounded base, clearly raised ribs, and a more prominent hub or plus. These are surface concepts, not complete moving switch mechanisms; the picture cannot establish the underside geometry.
- **Fantasy fruit with structure.** Every icon is an invented alien fruit. Use real fruit anatomy only as inspiration and vary skin structures rather than repeating the same pattern everywhere. Avoid stems and seeds. Focus on exteriors; earlier interior studies are archived.
- **Relief that belongs.** A plus may have a joined center and low ribs into its arms; refresh ribs follow the arrows; a gear's features radiate toward its teeth. Round junctions and keep the details integrated with the body. For a button fully supported underneath, these are primarily tactile and visual details: actual strength depends on geometry, materials, support, loads, and manufacturing. See [Protolabs on molded-rib geometry](https://www.protolabs.com/en-gb/resources/design-tips/design-essentials-for-injection-moulding/) and [Formlabs on 3D-print orientation and strength](https://formlabs.com/uk/blog/isotropy-in-SLA-3D-printing/).
- **Clean silhouettes.** Rind details and watermelon stripes should stay inside the edge and flow with curved shapes. Internal detail should not make a control ambiguous.
- **Color and light.** Use a pure black background and saturated candy colors, spreading distinct exterior hues evenly across the icon set. Use complementary or near-complementary accent hues with sufficient lightness contrast; do not rely on yellow for every secondary part. Keep highlights restrained and colored rather than washed out. Authored chromatic UI colors use 100% HSL saturation; generated concept art, interpolation, and antialiasing cannot guarantee that for every pixel.
- **Natural variation.** Some skins can have integrated color blushes, splotches or gradients and a few sparse sun freckles, while others can be uniform. Keep variations inside the clean silhouette and away from important part boundaries; they must not resemble seeds, bruises, or spoilage or diminish contrast.
- **Readable controls.** Test each finished icon in the actual interface at 12–16 px and 24 px and in its actual states. Meaningful UI graphics generally need at least 3:1 contrast with adjacent colors; normal text generally needs 4.5:1. Check the least-contrasting meaningful boundary across gradients after compositing, not just the endpoints or average. See the W3C's [non-text contrast guidance](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/non-text-contrast.html) and [text contrast guidance](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html). A palette check alone cannot establish conformance.

The raster concept sheets illustrate the direction; they are not final, pixel-perfect specifications or production icon files. The current study still needs the violet refresh texture aligned with the arrow's curve and more separation between the netted blue and purple skin treatments. Individual artwork, rendered contrast, and small-size legibility still need to be built and validated.
